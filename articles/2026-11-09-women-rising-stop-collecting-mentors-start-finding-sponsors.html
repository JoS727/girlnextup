Women Rising: Stop Collecting Mentors, Start Finding Sponsors

November 9, 2026

If your career has a bookshelf full of advice and no acceleration to show for it, the problem may be structural, not personal. Researchers and executives have converged on the same distinction for years, but most women are still coached as if it did not exist: a mentor talks to you; a sponsor talks about you. A mentor meets for coffee and helps you think. A sponsor spends political capital on you — in the room where the promotion list is drafted, the stretch project is assigned, the reorganization is sketched on a whiteboard you will never see. The difference between the two is roughly the difference between studying for a test and having someone slip the answer key into your jacket.

The research on why women end up over-mentored and under-sponsored is uncomfortable and specific. Studies from the Center for Talent Innovation and follow-on work published through the past two years have repeatedly found that women are at least as likely to have mentors as their peers — frequently more likely — but dramatically less likely to have advocates at the level where decisions are made. One reason is the modesty trap: women are trained to earn advocacy through flawless work and to feel presumptuous asking for it, while executive sponsors say plainly what they want in return — visible wins they can attach their name to. Another reason is sheer arithmetic of exposure. The senior leader who watches you present weekly becomes your advocate; the one who only hears about you in calibration meetings becomes, at best, your mentor.

So the practical question is not "how do I find a mentor?" but "how do I get seen doing something a decision-maker is willing to stake their reputation on?" The answer is rarely coffee. It is usually a project with risk and witness attached: a launch that could fail, a client escalation nobody wants, the cross-team initiative with three bosses' fingerprints on it. Sponsors are made in exactly those rooms, because sponsorship is a bet, and no one cashes a bet without a public payoff. Aim your next six months at a piece of work whose success cannot be missed, then ask the person who will be in the room when it is discussed a single question: "When this is evaluated, will you represent what it took?" That is not impertinence — that is the sponsorship contract spoken out loud, and executives respect having it made explicit.

There is a second move that works even earlier in a career, and almost nobody teaches it: borrow other people's sponsors before you have your own. The colleague who is trusted is trusted because someone vouches for her — and trust, studies of network dynamics show, travels along relationships far faster than résumés do. Being genuinely useful to one well-connected senior woman's flagship project puts you in the blast radius of her advocate's attention. You do not need to manufacture credibility from zero; you need to be adjacent to leverage long enough that the leverage notices you. Then, when that project concludes, the ask is small and specific — not "will you mentor me," which invites a coffee, but "would you be willing to mention this work to the committee," which invites a career.

The bookshelf, meanwhile, is not useless — but pick the one book that treats this as the operating problem rather than a footnote. Sylvia Ann Hewlett's Forget a Mentor, Find a Sponsor is still the clearest map of the mechanism, drawn from interviews with executives who admit, on the record, that sponsorship is how the top actually works. Read it less for inspiration than for its scripts — the exact conversations that convert a kind admirer into an investor in your trajectory.

And keep one caution in view: sponsorship without structure is charity, and charity is withdrawn quietly. The healthiest version of this fight is institutional. Ask your HR partner — in writing, gently, at review time — whether the company tracks sponsorship the way it now tracks mentorship, because the gap between the two programs is invisible in the aggregate and devastating in the disaggregated numbers. Companies that audit who gets promoted by referral versus who waits in the open queue tend to discover exactly the pattern the research predicts. Making that pattern visible is leadership work whether or not it is in your job description, and it is precisely the kind of work that gets the people who do it sponsored.

Mentors make you better. Sponsors make you faster. You need both, but only one of them is scarce enough to fight for. Put the coffee hours in their place this cycle, and go find the room where somebody is deciding whom to vouch for — then give them something vouchable to say about you.
