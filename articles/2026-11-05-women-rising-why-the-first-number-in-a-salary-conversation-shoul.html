Women Rising: Why the First Number in a Salary Conversation Should Be Yours

November 5, 2026

There is a moment in every offer call that quietly decides more money than any performance review ever will: the moment the first number enters the room. Whoever speaks it first sets the frame, and the frame does more work than the negotiation that follows. Behavioral researchers call it anchoring, and the effect is nearly rude in its strength — a higher starting figure pulls the entire conversation upward, because every subsequent number is judged relative to the one already on the table. When you quote first, the range under discussion is built around your value. When you wait, it is built around theirs. This is why so many professionals end up negotiating discounts off someone else's estimate of their worth, instead of letting the other side negotiate down from theirs.

The reason most capable women surrender the anchor is rarely a lack of information. It is a script. For generations the polite move was to ask what the role pays and respond from there, and that politeness has a measurable cost: the same negotiation, run from the other side of the table, produces materially different outcomes over a career. Compounding does the rest. A difference that looks small at hire — a few thousand, a slightly lower band — compounds through every later raise that is expressed as a percentage, every equity grant sized against current base, every next job that asks for your current salary as its starting point. The first number is not a moment in a conversation. It is the load-bearing wall of the next four years.

Building the Number Before the Call

An anchor only works if it is defensible, so build it before the call, not during it. Start with the market band for your role and level, from at least three independent sources, and then adjust for the specifics you bring: the systems only you can run, the customers who ask for you by name, the mess you inherited and fixed. Then decide your figure the way a pricing strategist would, not the way a hopeful applicant does. Quote at the top of what the evidence supports — not an absurd number that costs you credibility, but the top third of the honest range, stated evenly, followed by the two or three reasons it is correct. Silence after your number is a tool. State it, explain it briefly, and let the pause do the work you would be tempted to do by filling it with justifications.

Then prepare for the two deflections that always come. The first is the redirect: what are you making now? The clean answer treats history as irrelevant — a calm sentence about why the past role was priced differently and why you would like to focus on the value of this one. The second is the deferral: we have a budget. A budget is a negotiation position, not a law of physics, and the right response is curiosity rather than collapse: which part of the package is flexible, and what would move the number? Every deflection is the other side asking you to anchor yourself lower. The professionals who out-earn their peers are usually not the ones with better arguments — they are the ones who stopped answering deflections with their own devaluation.

Practice It Like a Skill, Not a Test

Negotiation is one of the few high-stakes professional skills that can be rehearsed alone, and almost nobody does. Record yourself saying the number and listen back — you are listening for the uptick, the apology tucked into the sentence, the rate of speech that speeds up under stress. A number delivered at normal conversational pace, without a laugh and without a rush, reads as a fact. One delivered quickly with a rising tone reads as an opening bid to be discounted. Run one live practice round with a friend playing a skeptical hiring manager; ten minutes of friction in a kitchen is worth more than an hour of reading negotiation advice, because the skill being trained is composure, not knowledge.

For a sharper study of how pressure, composure, and framing decide high-stakes conversations — and why the person who stays calmest usually shapes the outcome — the book on tactical negotiation that grew out of federal crisis negotiation work is worth a close read. Skip the theatrics; absorb the principle underneath: people move toward the frame they are handed first. Hand yourself one.

You will not win every anchor — some rooms are simply budgets in costume. But the habit of going first costs nothing and compounds everywhere it applies: the offer call, the consulting quote, the freelance rate, the raise conversation you scheduled after the results landed instead of before. The number you speak first is not greed. It is the first sentence of the story the room will tell about your value, and the professionals rising fastest have simply stopped letting someone else write it.
