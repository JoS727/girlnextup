Women Rising: The Confidence Myth and What Actually Holds Women Back

August 20, 2026 · Girl Next Up · 6 min read

For over a decade, the corporate self help industry has sold women the same solution to the same identified problem. You need more confidence. You need to lean in. You need to speak up more in meetings, negotiate harder, claim your space, and stop second guessing yourself. The messaging is everywhere. Books, conferences, webinars, and entire consulting practices have been built on the premise that if women would just believe in themselves a little more, the gender gap would close.

It sounds empowering. It is also a trap. The confidence gap narrative shifts the blame from systems onto individuals. It tells a woman who was passed over for a promotion that she should have been more assertive. It tells a founder who could not secure funding that she needed a better pitch. It tells a woman who got talked over in a meeting that she should have interrupted louder. The underlying message is always the same: the problem is you and your confidence. Fix that and the doors will open.

The data tells a different story.

The Competence Penalty

Research consistently shows that women are rated as more competent than men by their peers and managers. Performance reviews, 360 degree assessments, and peer evaluations all skew toward women receiving higher marks for actual work quality. And yet, women are promoted at lower rates, paid less for equivalent roles, and underrepresented in leadership positions.

If competence were the currency that purchased advancement, women would be running most organizations. The fact that they are not reveals the actual mechanism. Promotion is not a meritocracy. It is a social system that rewards visibility, proximity to power, and conformity to existing norms of behavior. And those norms were built by and for men.

The problem was never that women doubted themselves too much. The problem is that the system rewards performative confidence over demonstrated competence.

When a man says he can do something he has never done before, it is called ambition. When a woman says the same thing, it is called arrogance. When a man is direct, he is a strong leader. When a woman is direct, she is difficult. The same behavior produces opposite reactions because the behavior is not what is being evaluated. The person performing it is.

Imposter Syndrome Was a Label, Not a Diagnosis

The term imposter syndrome entered the mainstream conversation in the 1970s. Two psychologists, both women, described the phenomenon of high achieving women who could not internalize their own success. They attributed their accomplishments to luck, timing, or external factors rather than their own skill and effort.

The concept spread because it resonated. Women recognized themselves in it. But the framing was incomplete. What was labeled as imposter syndrome was often a rational response to real environmental signals. Women who were told, explicitly or implicitly, that they did not belong were not suffering from a syndrome. They were accurately reading the room.

If you tell a qualified woman she is not qualified enough times, she will start to believe it. That is not pathology. That is human cognition functioning normally under conditions of gaslighting. The problem is not that women doubt themselves. The problem is that the environment gives them reasons to doubt whether their work will be recognized.

What Actually Moves the Needle

If confidence is not the answer, what is? The research is clear on a few things that actually correlate with women advancing in their careers, and none of them involve taking a workshop on self belief.

First, structural changes to hiring and promotion processes produce results. When companies use structured interviews, blind resume reviews, and objective scoring rubrics, women get hired and promoted at higher rates. The removal of subjective judgment from the equation eliminates the space where bias lives. It is hard to penalize someone for being a woman when you cannot see who you are evaluating.

Second, sponsorship programs that pair women with senior leaders who actively advocate for their advancement work better than mentorship alone. A mentor helps you navigate the system. A sponsor changes the system on your behalf by putting your name forward for opportunities you would not otherwise be considered for.

Third, pay transparency closes gaps. When salaries are visible, women negotiate more effectively and employers cannot hide disparities behind the veil of confidentiality. The states and countries that have mandated pay transparency have seen measurable reductions in gender pay gaps.

The Stories We Tell Matter

The narratives we consume shape what we believe is possible. When the dominant story about women and work is that women need fixing, that is where the energy goes. Workshops, books, and coaching sessions designed to make women more confident proliferate while the actual structures that hold them back remain untouched.

Changing the story changes the strategy. When women stop asking what is wrong with them and start asking what is wrong with the system, the work shifts from self improvement to institutional change. Both are necessary. But only one of them moves the needle at scale.

For anyone who wants to understand the research behind these dynamics and how to actually dismantle bias in workplace systems, Bias Interrupted by Joan C. Williams is a practical, evidence based guide that goes beyond individual fixes and addresses the structural problems that keep holding women back.

Stop Fixing Women

The next time you hear someone say women need more confidence, ask what they mean. Do they mean women need to behave more like men in a system designed to reward masculine norms? Or do they mean the system should be redesigned to recognize competence regardless of the form it takes?

Women are not rising because they finally found their confidence. They are rising because they are building the networks, launching the companies, writing the research, and demanding the structural changes that make advancement possible without pretending to be someone they are not.

The confidence myth was always a distraction. The real work is not internal. It is structural. And women are already doing it.
