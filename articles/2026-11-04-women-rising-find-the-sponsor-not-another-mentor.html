Women Rising: Find the Sponsor, Not Another Mentor

November 4, 2026

Quick vocabulary check before the next networking hour costs you another evening: a mentor gives you perspective, and a sponsor gives you opportunity. A mentor talks through your career with you. A sponsor talks about your career without you in the room — to the people who decide who gets the stretch work, who gets the introduction, who gets remembered when a role opens. The distinction matters now more than ever, because the title economy has caught up to the concept: every leadership conference has a session on sponsorship, everyone agrees it is important, and yet most talented women still cannot name their own sponsor. They can name three people whose doors are always open. That is not the same thing. That is a fan club.

Look for receipts instead of feelings. A genuine sponsor sends opportunities to you before they are posted anywhere. She says your name with proof points attached when you are not present: led the migration, rebuilt the forecast, fixed the churn problem nobody would own. He hands you a project that costs him something to give, not a project that only costs you something to do. She prepares the room before you walk into it — and then tells you afterward exactly what she said. Sponsorship is measurable behavior, usually invisible to you, performed by someone with power to spend. That last clause is the one people skip. A supportive peer is a gift; a supportive peer with influence over your next role is something else entirely.

The Five Checks Before Review Season

Run each advocate on your list through five questions. One: have they spent anything — political capital, budget, headcount, their own seniority — on your advancement in the past six months? Two: have they put your name forward for an opportunity you did not already know about? Three: do they know your numbers, the concrete outcomes you shipped, well enough to repeat them without you in the room? Four: have they told you what they said about you, or asked you directly what you want next? Five: do they sit at a level above the decisions you want made about you? One yes with receipts is a beginning. Three or more is a sponsor. Five is a person you should be protecting with the same care they extend to you.

Scored low? That is information, not indictment. The move is not to mourn your mentors — keep them, mentorship matters deeply — but to build sponsorship deliberately, because it almost never happens by osmosis. Sponsorship attaches to performance that is witnessed, and most excellent work by women, structurally, is performed where the witnesses are few: the unglamorous systems project, the client save nobody saw, the cleanup after someone else's ambition. Change the geography. Pick one high-visibility deliverable per quarter and treat it as staged work: chosen because the right people will see it, scoped because it can actually complete, and narrated while it happens, not after. Send the update email with the metric in the subject line. You are not bragging; you are handing your future sponsor her evidence.

Then run the playbook in the other direction and sponsor someone earlier than feels reasonable. Write the peer's impact into the promotion packet when she is pulled away. Say her name with numbers, out loud, in the meeting that matters. Sponsorship culture is not something you wait to be handed — it is something you seed, and it compounds: the women who lift each other with receipts build networks that circulate power, not just encouragement. The mentored get advice. The sponsored get slots. Somewhere in a hiring or promo meeting next quarter, someone will say the word that changes your trajectory, and it will be your name. Your job this season is to make sure the sentence that follows reads like evidence.

For a sharper study of how advocacy and perception quietly shape who advances — and why talent alone has never been the whole mechanism — this landmark book on ambition and visibility is worth a careful read. It is not inspiration. It is pattern recognition for a career.

Audit the room the way you would audit a project: receipts, spend, outcomes. Keep the mentors. Find the sponsor. And once you have one, remember what it felt like to be the talented woman nobody was naming — then go be someone’s name in that meeting.
